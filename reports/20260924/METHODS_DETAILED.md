# Methods

## Design, provenance and analysis units

We conducted an exploratory, non-preregistered secondary analysis of open human EEG datasets and a separate simulation study. No new participants were recruited. The analyses address four distinct questions: whether trajectory summaries predict retrospective reports of experience in held-out sleep studies; whether any predictive pattern depends on labels or representation-level temporal order; whether similar summaries vary with experimental context and global subjective ratings; and whether finite observation affects the estimators and their interval coverage. Perturbation and sensory-report datasets provide separate construct-boundary analyses. We did not treat the different reports or stimulation proxies as interchangeable measures of consciousness.

The principal independent generalization unit in the sleep analysis was the source study group. Observations were nested within participant identifiers, which were nested within studies. The sparse and higher-density portfolios overlap in source material and must not be pooled as independent samples. Algorithmic null refits are not biological replicates. Other branches use paired participants as their resampling or sign-flip units. Dataset and code identities were recorded by source release and SHA-256 checksums; historical outputs were preserved when the 5,000-refit continuation was run. The final statistical tables were exported without raw EEG, questionnaire records or participant-level predictions.

## Sleep cohorts and outcomes

The sparse core used eligible stage-N2 awakenings from Oslo, Turku and Wisconsin, comprising 80 participant identifiers and 386 observations. The principal contrast compared reports of experience with recall against explicit reports of no experience. Experience without recall was examined separately rather than assumed to lie on an ordinal scale. Oslo evening and morning recordings were held out as one study group because possible participant aliases across those collections remained unresolved. Counts therefore denote identifiers, not guaranteed distinct people across all releases. A numerical category was admitted only when its source documentation established the report meaning.

The higher-density extension comprised 62 identifiers and 380 observations across Oslo, Turku and Rome Sapienza. It reused eligible Oslo and Turku material and added Rome's binary experience reports. Rome's positive category does not establish recalled dream content, and ambiguous reports were excluded. The extension also conservatively excluded nonempty source quality remarks. Its report assay and cohort composition therefore differ from the sparse core. Comparing significance between portfolios cannot identify the causal effect of electrode count.

The sparse montage contained Fp1, C3 and O1. The extension used Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, F7, F8, C3, C4, Cz, P3, P4, Pz, O1 and O2. Wisconsin positions were mapped to observed HydroCel sensors with documented offsets of approximately 1.0-1.3 cm; no missing channel was interpolated. Named channels were used in other collections, with the source-supported Rome alias `01` interpreted as O1. Strict montage, report-category and stage variants remained in the analysis inventory, including unavailable analyses with fewer than three independent study groups.

## Sleep EEG preprocessing and representations

We extracted the final 20 s before each documented awakening boundary. Constant EDF padding was removed only under the explicit suffix rule, and recordings with excessive terminal flat runs were excluded. Selected observed channels were average-referenced, filtered from 0.5 to 30 Hz with a fourth-order zero-phase Butterworth filter, and resampled to 200 Hz. Finite stretches were filtered separately so that internal gaps did not become synthetic transitions. Non-overlapping 1-s windows were rejected for nonfinite samples, peak-to-peak amplitude greater than 500 microvolts in any selected channel, or channel standard deviation below 0.01 microvolts. At least 10 clean seconds were required. Rejected windows split contiguous segments; computations requiring temporal adjacency did not bridge gaps. Because filtering was offline and zero-phase, these results do not establish prospective prediction latency.

Three representation families were evaluated separately. Sensor features were channel-wise log root-mean-square amplitude per second. Time-frequency features were channel-wise log Welch power in delta (0.5-4 Hz), theta (4-8 Hz), alpha (8-13 Hz) and beta (13-30 Hz) bands, with half-open band boundaries. The frozen LaBraM-base encoder received 200-sample microvolt patches; its weights were not fine-tuned to the experience labels. The implementation was pinned to revision `c431221e6cfd23dbfa9950e0180682fb322b0548`. Possible overlap between upstream pretraining and the present cohorts could not be fully excluded, so encoder results are not described as transfer to definitively unseen pretraining populations.

## Trajectory profile and comparators

Within each training partition, weighted covariance principal components reduced the representation to two coordinates, scaled by the training eigenvalues. A three-state k-means dictionary was fitted with 10 initializations. Training weights balanced studies and participant identifiers and were distributed over the time points of each observation. The fitted mapping was applied unchanged to validation and held-out observations. Fitting this transform separately inside each inner and outer training partition prevented test data from determining the dictionary.

The eight-feature trajectory profile contained repertoire, log median dwell, dwell dispersion, recurrence, exit entropy, directionality, alignment and reachability. Repertoire was the participation ratio of the shrinkage covariance eigenvalues. Dwell runs ended at a state change or segment boundary; log dwell was the natural logarithm of the median run duration, and dwell dispersion was the interquartile range divided by the median. Recurrence was the fraction of eligible subsequent runs returning to a state previously visited within the same segment; each segment's first run was excluded from its denominator. Exit entropy summarized normalized destination-state diversity, weighted by exit count. Directionality was a transition-based entropy-production estimate. Alignment used rank-one, two-fold cross-validated ridge prediction between available regional representations while preserving segment boundaries. Reachability was a regularized local-linear proxy in the reduced state space, weighted by occupancy; it is not intervention-based physical controllability. Nonfinite or failed estimators were recorded as unavailable, not replaced by favourable values.

Conventional features were calculated in contiguous 1-s signal windows and averaged within observation: relative delta, theta, alpha and beta power; the 2-30 Hz spectral exponent; normalized Lempel-Ziv complexity; permutation entropy; and log root-mean-square amplitude. The multivariate conventional comparator used these eight features. A spectral comparator used the four relative powers and exponent. A nonlinear scalar comparator expanded Lempel-Ziv complexity to polynomial degree three. These comparators distinguish improvement over a linear multivariate benchmark from improvement over a flexible model of one complexity measure.

## Nested prediction, reference model and precision

The outer evaluation left one independent study group out. Hyperparameters were chosen by group-held-out validation in the remaining studies; with three groups overall, each outer fold had two inner training-validation splits. Every data-fitted transform, imputation, standardization, classifier and model-selection step was confined to its relevant training partition. Models used L2-regularized logistic regression. The trajectory grid crossed inverse-regularization strengths 0.1, 1 and 10 with profile dimensions 1-5. Comparator grids used 15 log-spaced strengths from 0.1 to 10, matching the number of requested tuning evaluations. The nonlinear scalar expansion followed training-fitted scaling. Training-mean imputation was used, and entirely unmeasured training features were dropped.

Log loss was the main statistic. Observation losses were averaged within participant identifier, then within held-out study; study means received equal weight. Paired effects were trajectory loss minus comparator loss, so negative values favour trajectories. AUROC, Brier score and average precision were secondary diagnostics. A post-results constant reference assigned each held-out study the mean experience rate estimated only from the other studies, with the same participant/study balancing. Held-out labels never set its probability. This reference was descriptive; no paired uncertainty interval was added for its contrast with trajectories.

Original 95% prediction intervals used 1,000 hierarchical bootstrap draws, resampling studies and participant-level mean paired losses within study. These intervals condition on fitted held-out predictions; model selection and fitting were not repeated inside the bootstrap. A precision sensitivity used the three study-mean paired differences with a Student-t multiplier and two degrees of freedom, plus delete-one-study averages. Shared training across outer folds and only three studies prevent either approach from serving as a fully calibrated population-generalization interval. An interval crossing zero was not interpreted as equivalence; no retrospective observed power was calculated.

## Null-refit controls and multiplicity

For each portfolio and representation, three controls were applied. Label permutation shuffled experience labels within study, participant identifier and sleep stage. Temporal permutation changed the order of representation samples within each intact segment, using a common ordering for the full trajectory and regional arrays. Representation-phase controls independently randomized Fourier phases within segments while preserving the corresponding representation spectra. The latter two controls act on representations, not on the original EEG signal, and should not be described as original-signal spectral nulls. Each refit repeated the complete nested prediction pipeline.

An initial 100 seeded refits per null distribution were completed. Their minimum attainable plus-one p-value could not pass a 72-test Holm family. After those results had been inspected, a fixed extension to 5,000 refits per distribution was chosen and completed for every family. Observed analyses were unchanged, but the decision is post-results and does not convert this work into a preregistered test. The final family comprised two portfolios x three representations x three controls x four statistics: absolute trajectory loss and paired differences versus conventional, nonlinear-scalar and spectral comparators. For each lower-is-better statistic we calculated `p = [1 + count(null statistic <= observed statistic)] / 5,001`, then Holm-adjusted all 72 p-values together. The minimum possible raw p is 1/5,001. Four corrected tests can arise from the same representation and control and are not four independent biological replications. Label exchangeability is an assumption; only 49/80 core and 29/62 extension participant blocks contained both observed labels and could actually permute.

## Context and subjective measures

PsiConnect EEG came from the public OpenNeuro release ds006110 v1.2.1. We used its cleaned FieldTrip baseline and psilocybin-session files for rest, meditation, music and movie contexts, selecting 14 observed channels matching the extension montage. Each file contributed up to its first 120 accepted 1-s windows, with at least 10 required. After observed-channel average reference, 0.5-30 Hz filtering and 200-Hz resampling, nonfinite/flat windows and windows with an absolute-amplitude-to-global-RMS ratio above 30 were excluded. Gaps defined segment boundaries. Because the source units could not be verified for encoder input, accepted signals were divided by global RMS; this branch concerns amplitude-normalized sensor trajectories only.

For each held-out participant, the trajectory dictionary was fitted on baseline recordings from other participants. Eight trajectory features were standardized with training-baseline means and standard deviations; Lempel-Ziv complexity remained in native units. We calculated session change within each context as session 02 minus session 01. A context-versus-rest interaction was the context's session change minus the same participant's rest change. Original descriptive intervals used 1,000 participant-bootstrap draws, holding the cross-fitted features fixed. A subsequent audit retained all 36 session changes and 27 interactions. Two-sided plus-one sign-flip p-values used 19,999 participant-level draws and Holm correction over all 63 tests; sign flips assume symmetric participant differences under the null. Feature construction and shared normalization were not refitted during these tests. Fixed task order, eye-state differences and no placebo session limit causal interpretation.

All 11 original, non-imputed ASC11 subscales were obtained from the public session-02 phenotype derivatives at pinned repository revision `8c5d6b077c63d29796409b622ebc300fdec6aadd`. Participant IDs were joined exactly after removal of an optional prefix, never by row order. For four contexts, nine EEG features and 11 subscales, 396 Spearman correlations related within-person EEG session change to global post-session ratings. Missing scores remained missing; complete-pair counts ranged from 47 to 53. Asymptotic rank-correlation p-values were Holm-adjusted across all 396 comparisons. Descriptive 95% intervals used 999 participant-pair bootstrap draws and were not simultaneous. Global ratings were not treated as simultaneous context-specific experience reports.

## Finite-observation simulations

A separate known-parameter stationary three-state Markov model diagnosed the behaviour of recurrence, exit entropy and dwell dispersion. Initial states were uniform. Stay probabilities were 0.3 or 0.7; conditional on moving, either alternative state was chosen equally. Sequence lengths were 12, 20 or 120, with independently deleted fractions of 0 or 0.2. The 12 settings therefore crossed length, persistence and missingness. Deletions created gaps rather than transitions.

For each setting, 1,000 independent sequences estimated the finite-window mean target. A separate 1,000-sequence calibration set estimated errors around that target, and 200 independent test sequences evaluated interval coverage. A continuous 10,000-state realization supplied a distinct long-record reference. For each test sequence, the original procedure produced 99 length-matched resamples using blocks of three observed samples; original and resampled discontinuities remained segment boundaries. Coverage was computed against both finite-window and long-record targets. The known-model interval used empirical finite-window error quantiles. Exact binomial intervals describe Monte Carlo coverage uncertainty. These diagnostics are not replacement EEG intervals and do not validate all eight trajectory features. The broader recovery inventory additionally records bias, RMSE, coverage and unavailable cells over eight axes.

## Perturbation, tactile and report-task checks

In the propofol/TMS dataset ds005620 v1.0.0, passive and stimulation recordings were paired by exact participant, session, run and task entities. Available passive sensor features were added to conventional predictors and a sedation indicator. Nested participant-held-out ridge regression selected regularization from 0.1, 1 and 10. The three outcomes were sensor-spread fraction, response differentiation (covariance participation ratio of the averaged 20-300-ms response) and the last post-20-ms crossing of a baseline-derived global-field-power threshold. Right-censored recovery records were excluded from recovery prediction. Participant-mean paired squared-error differences and conditional participant bootstrap intervals assessed the incremental model. These response measures are stimulation proxies, not direct tests of the reachability estimator. Paired awake-sedation change summaries have only two or three participants; individual paired predictions were excluded from the exported package.

The near-threshold tactile dataset ds001785 v1.1.1 and an open somatosensory report-task resource used a separate subsecond sensor track. Baseline-normalized RMS, spatial participation of mean sensor responses and temporal roughness were calculated in intervals -200-0, 20-80, 80-200, 200-400 and 400-600 ms relative to stimulation. A causal fourth-order 40-Hz low-pass filter was applied, although upstream preprocessing may still preclude prospective timing claims. Conditions were matched on physical intensity within participant before averaging. For each eligible contrast, participant-level means were bootstrapped and sign-flipped 1,000 times; two-sided plus-one p-values were Holm-adjusted within the recorded comparison family. Confidence associations were within-participant slopes stratified by intensity and detection, not total effects. Missing responses stayed missing. Tactile pre-response contrasts required the entire feature window to end before the recorded response. One checksum-valid but internally incomplete EEG recording was excluded whole. Report-task condition order and task relevance limit consciousness-specific interpretation.

## Missing analyses, reproducibility and reporting

The analysis inventory records each attempted transfer, sensitivity and robustness cell as estimated, analysed, unavailable or lacking a summary. An unavailable cell was not set to zero or silently omitted. Source tables, figure panels and report claims retain their distinct inferential units and correction families: 72 null controls, 63 context boundary tests, 396 subjective associations and the sensory families reported separately. Exact aggregate outputs and the full 5,000-refit statistics are linked in the versioned export manifest; source-derived participant-level values and raw recordings are not redistributed. No single corrected result establishes a conserved manifold or a clinical biomarker.
